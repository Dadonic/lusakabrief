'We are scared of foreign investments': in Kitwe, Musokotwane says fear of outside capital holds Africa back, and defends the IMF as a US$1.47 billion deal awaits approval

At the Copperbelt 2027 National Budget Symposium in Kitwe, Finance Minister Situmbeko Musokotwane said that China, Japan and Germany grew by opening up to foreign investment and that investors create jobs for local firms rather than crowding them out, News Diggers reported on Wednesday 18 November. The Zambian Observer reported him saying mineral wealth is no reason to leave the IMF, whose 191 members include Saudi Arabia, after Zambia's US$1.7 billion facility closed in January and a new US$1.472 billion arrangement reached staff level in October.

By Lusaka Brief Business Desk • KITWE — 19 November 2026 • 2 min read

Finance and National Planning Minister Situmbeko Musokotwane took the 2027 budget to the Copperbelt this week and used the occasion to confront a strain of economic nationalism he thinks is costing the continent. Speaking at the Copperbelt 2027 National Budget Symposium in Kitwe, he said that one of the reasons Africa lags other regions is a fear of foreign investment, News Diggers reported on Wednesday 18 November.

One of the reasons Africa is lagging behind is because we are scared of foreign investments.
— Dr Situmbeko Musokotwane, Minister of Finance and National Planning, at the Copperbelt 2027 National Budget Symposium in Kitwe, as reported by News Diggers on 18 November

His argument, as the paper reported it, was historical: China, Japan and Germany all grew by opening their economies to outside capital at a time when they lacked enough of their own. He rejected the view that foreign investors damage local business, saying they create jobs and opportunities for local entrepreneurs. The theme runs through the 2027 budget itself, which Zambia Monitor reported on 7 November sets a copper production target of 930,000 tonnes for next year on the road to three million tonnes a year by 2031, leaning on expansions at First Quantum, Barrick Lumwana, Konkola and Mopani and on the greenfield Kitumba and KoBold projects, with a promise of a stable and predictable mining tax regime.

Mineral wealth and the Fund

The minister also answered the argument that a copper-rich country has no business with the International Monetary Fund. In remarks carried by the Zambian Observer on Wednesday, he said the IMF has 191 members, including the world's largest economies and major oil producers, and pointed to Saudi Arabia, whose finance minister, Mohammed Aljadaan, chairs the Fund's International Monetary and Financial Committee. Zambia completed the final review of its Extended Credit Facility in January 2026, with cumulative disbursements of about US$1.7 billion, and in October reached a staff-level agreement on a new 36-month arrangement of about US$1.472 billion, which still needs the approval of the IMF board. He tied the approach to the 2027 budget and the Grow Zambia Agenda, summarising it as "partnerships where necessary, national ownership always, and development benefits for all".

The mines ministry's conditions

The same week, Mines Minister Brenda Tambatamba told a breakfast meeting with stakeholders on Tuesday that anti-corruption measures would be non-negotiable standards in her ministry as it pursued the three-million-tonne target, and that government would work with mining companies, investors, communities and civil society on illegal mining and pollution, News Diggers reported. "As a ministry, we realise that we cannot effect the much-needed change alone," the paper quoted her as saying.

Not every minister's emphasis was on openness. Industry Minister Noel Nkhoma, receiving the Zambia Development Agency's director general Albert Halwampa, called for an industrialisation master plan and a master plan for the Lobito Corridor, and warned that dumping was eroding local manufacturers' market share, Zambia Monitor reported on Tuesday 17 November. "We need to capacitate and protect our industries because that is where the jobs and budget support are coming from," he said. Halwampa suggested using World Trade Organisation mechanisms against unfair trade rather than working in isolation.
