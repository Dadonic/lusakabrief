Tenge Tenge, TK and a sold-out Golden Circle: Jason Derulo's only African stop turned the Showgrounds into the festival the continent talked about

Saturday's Stanbic Music Festival finale at Polo Field A was Jason Derulo's only African stop on The Last Dance World Tour — and he shared the bill with Yo Maps, Mampi, Danny Kaya and a 13-year-old Ugandan viral sensation. The Culture Desk looks back at the weekend Lusaka's festival economy came of age, and forward to the heritage week ahead.

By Lusaka Brief Culture Desk • LUSAKA — 8 October 2026 • 5 min read

The lights stayed up past midnight, and Lusaka earned every one of them. Jason Derulo's headline set at the Stanbic Music Festival on Saturday night — the only African stop on his The Last Dance World Tour — closed a two-day takeover of Polo Field A at the Lusaka Showgrounds that organisers, artists and government are all, for once, describing with the same word: landmark. The American hitmaker gave the sell-out crowd the catalogue without pause — "Talk Dirty", "Want To Want Me", "Wiggle", "Savage Love" — backed by his own dancers and a live band, on a stage built to touring-standard specification.

The night belonged to more than its headliner. Yo Maps, Mampi and Danny Kaya carried the home bill — Kaya producing the evening's loudest gasp when he brought out TK, the legendary producer behind a generation of Zambian hits — with Mwila and the Band and Zimbabwe's Tamy Moyo in support and the DJs keeping the field moving between sets. The moment that will outlive the setlists belonged to neither headliner nor legend: Tenge Tenge, the 13-year-old Ugandan internet sensation, was initially blocked by security when he moved toward the stage — until Derulo himself intervened and brought the boy up, later dancing alongside a Zambian fan, Judy, whose Chikok moves had caught his eye. A world tour's only African date ended with a Ugandan child and a Lusaka fan on its stage. Festival programming cannot buy that.

The economics deserve their own paragraph. Standard tickets went at K800 and VIP at K2,500, and the Golden Circle sold out days before the gates opened — demand the festival matched to a purpose through its Tickets for Trees campaign, under which every ticket sold plants a tree. Festival chairperson Ngoza Nyirenda framed the weekend as proof that Lusaka can stage world-standard productions; government voices, from the Ministry of Youth, Sport and Arts' Kangwa Chileshe to Tourism's Evans Muhanga, reached for the creative-economy language the administration has made policy — festivals not as parties but as GDP. Stanbic chief executive Mwindwa Siakalima's version was the banker's: culture is now an investment class.

The week ahead keeps the calendar warm. The stage production "And… Action!" was staged on Wednesday to open the heritage-season run, and the National Theatre Arts Association of Zambia marks forty years with a week of events in Chingola from 19 to 24 October. The Culture Desk's verdict on the weekend: for one night, the Showgrounds was the most international room in Africa — and the home acts were not the support, they were the point. Heritage month now has to prove the echo lasts longer than the ringing in our ears.
