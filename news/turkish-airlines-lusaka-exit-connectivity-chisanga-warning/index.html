Turkish Airlines is leaving Lusaka's map — and an economist says the route is the least of what departs with it

The carrier has removed Lusaka from its future schedules for good. Kelvin Chisanga's warning is about what goes with it: investor access, tourist arrivals, and a piece of the country's argument — made again in Abu Dhabi this week — that Zambia is open for business.

By Natasha Banda • LUSAKA — 30 September 2026 • 4 min read

Airlines rarely announce a departure twice. Turkish Airlines has removed Lusaka from its future schedule — permanently, according to the reporting this week — and with that one scheduling decision, Zambia loses a link to one of the world's largest networks and the hub logic of Istanbul, through which a Lusaka ticket could reach more countries than any other single connection on the departure board.

Kelvin Chisanga, a Lusaka-based economist, reads the withdrawal as a diagnostic, not a timetable change. It is, he told media this week, "a serious warning about Zambia's economic connectivity and the strength of the demand that the country is creating for global markets and opportunities." His argument runs through three channels. For investors, connectivity sets the cost and convenience of reaching projects and partners. For tourism, it decides how easily visitors reach the destinations — against a record of 2.19 million international arrivals in 2024, up 58 per cent on 2023, momentum that needs seats in the air to sustain it. And for the macro story, the exit lands at an awkward altitude: GDP grew 7.2 per cent in the second quarter of 2026 and inflation fell to 6.2 per cent in August, yet the demand those numbers should generate has not, in this case, kept a global carrier on the route.

Chisanga's prescription is a National Air Connectivity Strategy — aviation linked directly to investment, tourism and trade policy rather than left to route-by-route arithmetic. The timing gives the idea its edge. This is the week the President stood in Abu Dhabi selling Zambia as open, reformed and ready — and the week one of the Gulf's rival hubs lost its own direct link to Lusaka. Route economics are unsentimental: carriers fly where yields live. But a country whose pitch is three million tonnes of copper, a medical city and a tourism brand called "Zambia Moves You" needs the world to be able to move to Zambia. The government cannot subsidise every route; it can, however, decide whether losing one is a market event or a policy failure. That is the question the departure board leaves behind.
