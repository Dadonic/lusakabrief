Politics · The Banda case

A night at Chimbokaila, a day of paperwork, then a smile at the gate: the Archbishop walks free on bail

The Archbishop of Lusaka spent Monday night in the capital's best-known correctional facility while his lawyers worked the bail conditions through the system. Late on Tuesday afternoon the gates opened, and the country's most watched defendant walked out smiling — to high-fives, handshakes and a Church that had barely slept.

By Chanda Mwape • LUSAKA — 30 September 2026 • 5 min read

The question this newspaper left hanging on Tuesday morning — whether the Archbishop of Lusaka had slept at home or in a cell — now has its answer. He slept at Chimbokaila. And on Tuesday evening, after a full day of bail formalities, the Most Reverend Dr Alick Banda walked out of the Lusaka Central Correctional Facility a free man awaiting trial, smiling and trading high-fives, handshakes and quick hugs with the small crowd of clergy, lawyers and faithful who had kept vigil at the gates.

The sequence, as reconstructed from court and prison precincts, runs like this. Bail was granted on Monday afternoon — K80,000 in his own recognisance, three sureties at K50,000 each, travel documents surrendered. But sureties must be interviewed, verified and bonded, and that machinery ran slowly. As late as 14:50 on Tuesday the process was still grinding, with court officials verifying the sureties' details. By then the Archbishop had already spent one night in custody — a night the opposition seized on, and one the Archdiocese met with silence rather than statements. Late in the afternoon the paperwork was done; the gates opened; and the images that will define this phase of the case — a metropolitan archbishop beaming outside a prison — were captured within minutes.

What happened inside the process is where the accounts diverge. Allies of the Archbishop, most vocally former ambassador Emmanuel Mwamba, describe a day of obstruction — officials, he says, repeatedly challenged the details of the sureties while a bailed man waited in a cell. The court record shows something more mundane: bail conditions with verification requirements being processed across a day and a half. Both can be true at once, and neither has been tested anywhere but on social media. What is established is the timeline: charged Monday, court Monday, custody Monday night, release Tuesday evening.

The politics did not pause for the paperwork. The bishops' conference has kept its language careful — dignity, due process, no prejudice or intimidation — while the opposition continues to frame the arrest as persecution and the State continues to frame it as a vehicle. The arrest has travelled: regional and international outlets covered it within hours, and the framing they carried — a government critic in the dock — is the framing Lusaka most wanted to avoid in the fortnight before the UN Human Rights Council vote on 13 October. Against that, the State can now point to a completed due-process cycle: summons, charge, plea, bail, release, dates.

Those dates are the story's spine from here. The matter returns for mention on 14 October; trial is scheduled to open on 3 November. The Archbishop's defence team — State counsels John Sangwa and Bonaventure Mutale — insist he is innocent, and the prosecution, led at Monday's appearance by the DPP himself, must by November turn a five-year-old paper trail about a blue Hilux into proof under Section 319. The country, meanwhile, has learned something it did not know on Sunday: that its institutions will, in fact, detain an archbishop overnight — and release him on schedule. What that proves, and to whom, is the argument Zambia will be having all October.

Confirmed vs. alleged

Confirmed: Archbishop Banda was granted bail on Monday with verification requirements for three sureties; he remained in custody overnight and was released from the Lusaka Central Correctional Facility late on Tuesday after the bail process was completed; he appeared relaxed and greeted supporters at the gate; the case returns for mention on 14 October, with trial scheduled for 3 November.

Alleged / contested: That officials deliberately frustrated the surety process to prolong the detention (asserted by Emmanuel Mwamba and opposition figures; not confirmed by the court, the DEC or the Archdiocese); that the prosecution amounts to persecution (the opposition's and some clergy members' characterisation, rejected by the government, the UPND and the DEC).
