Double the demand, stuck at the wires: capacity hits 4,722MW — and the President names the grid as the laggard

President Hichilema has told the Energy Forum for Africa that Zambia's installed generation capacity of 4,722.3MW is now nearly twice peak national demand of about 2,400MW — but warned that the transmission and distribution network is lagging behind and must attract urgent investment.

By Natasha Banda • LUSAKA — 9 October 2026 • 4 min read

The country can now make nearly twice the power it needs — the problem is moving it. President Hakainde Hichilema, in a speech delivered on his behalf by Vice-President Mutale Nalumango at the third Energy Forum for Africa in Lusaka, put the installed generation figure at 4,722.3MW, up from 3,777MW in 2024, against a peak national demand of about 2,400MW, according to Ministry of Energy data. The surplus is the headline; the warning was the substance: "although we have recorded positive gains and investment in the generation space, transmission and distribution space is lagging behind."

The composition of the build-out explains both the achievement and the bottleneck. Solar capacity has jumped from 178MW in 2024 to 1,027.5MW — about 21.76 percent of the installed total, and past the Government's 1,000MW-by-2026 target with months to spare — driven largely by independent producers who can erect a solar farm far faster than a utility can string a high-voltage line. The drought of 2024/25 taught the country what an over-hydro grid costs; the build-out since has been deliberate insurance. But generation without evacuation is inventory: power that exists on paper at the plant gate and never reaches a smelter, a farm or a home.

The President's answer is a call for capital into the unglamorous half of the sector — transmission lines, substations and distribution — plus the regional interconnectors that would let a borderless African power market buy Zambia's surplus. Behind that sit the mega-projects the forum heard about: Batoka Gorge's 2,400MW and Devil's Gorge's 2,380MW on the Zambezi, and a 1,000MW floating solar scheme on Lake Kariba. The context that gives the appeal its edge is the mines: the Treasury's growth story banks on copper output climbing, and every new shaft draws from the same grid. Zambia has spent two years proving it can build generation quickly; the 2031 target of 10,000MW will be decided by whether it can build wires at the same speed.
