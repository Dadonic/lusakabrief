The ceremony that fed a revolution: Samu Lya Moomba returns to Bweengwa — a reminder that a nation is built by sacrifice, not political songs

Thousands gathered in Chief Choongo's country at the weekend for the 2026 Samu Lya Moomba traditional ceremony. Beneath the drums and the season's politics sits an older claim on the national memory: parliamentary records remember Bweengwa as the place where villagers contributed cattle to fund the independence struggle.

By Lusaka Brief Culture Desk • MONZE — 6 October 2026 • 4 min read

All roads to Monze District led to Chief Choongo's country this weekend. The 2026 Samu Lya Moomba traditional ceremony of the Tonga people drew thousands to Bweengwa across Saturday and Sunday — government officials, traditional leaders, corporate executives and local residents — with President Hichilema attending his first traditional ceremony since his re-election, and using the ground to deliver both a drought warning and a pledge that traditional ceremonies should mobilise communities around agriculture, entrepreneurship and production.

The ground has heard heavier history. As The Zambian Observer's ceremony essay recalls, Samu Lya Moomba's claim on the nation is written in parliamentary records going back many years: Bweengwa as the place where people gathered, contributed cattle and mobilised resources so that freedom fighters — Harry Mwaanga Nkumbula, Kenneth Kaunda among them — could advance the struggle for independence. The essay's thesis is a rebuke wrapped in a celebration: a nation is built by sacrifice, not political songs. In a season when the country's politics runs hot, the ceremony's deeper register is that its hosts were funding liberation before there were parties to sing for.

That is the quiet power of the traditional-ceremony calendar, of which Samu Lya Moomba is among the most politically attended: it is the one series of gatherings that belongs to no administration. Chiefs host; governments come as guests; the dancing, the praise poetry and the cattle lines proceed on Tonga time, not Lusaka time. This year's edition carried the extra weight of an El Niño warning delivered from its podium — proof, if any were needed, that the ceremonies are now among the country's most effective civic infrastructure, the route by which a message reaches the village without passing through a party structure.

The Culture Desk's note for the season is simple. Between August's election and October's court dates, the country's public life has narrowed to winners and losers. Bweengwa's weekend offered a longer lens: a people who once paid for a nation in cattle, hosting the fifth edition of its government. The drums did not resolve anything. They did something older — they reminded everyone present of what the ground has already survived.
