Economy · Cost of living

K12,253.72 to keep a Lusaka family of five: the JCTR basket jumps K854 in a month, and the centre says the budget should have taxed copper windfalls, not wage earners

The Jesuit Centre for Theological Reflection's Basic Needs and Nutrition Basket for Lusaka rose 7.5% in October, from K11,399.64 to K12,253.72, driven by vegetables and bananas in the hot season. Its director, Fr Daniel Mutale, says formal workers already carry the heaviest tax load and that the budget's VAT and mobile-money increases will reach the poor; PwC, meanwhile, says households will feel the budget at the till and wants a timetable for K60 billion in unpaid VAT refunds.

By Lusaka Brief Business Desk • LUSAKA — 12 November 2026 • 3 min read

The cost of meeting a Lusaka family's basic needs rose by K854.08 in October to K12,253.72, the Jesuit Centre for Theological Reflection said in its monthly Basic Needs and Nutrition Basket, published on Tuesday 10 November. That is a 7.5% jump in a single month, Zambia Monitor reported, and most of it came from food: the food component rose from K4,574.79 to K5,339.52. News Diggers reported that the centre's executive director, Fr Daniel Mutale, attributed the rise mainly to vegetables and bananas, and the Daily Nation gave the figures: a 40kg bag of vegetables went from K763.97 to K1,122.57 and a kilogram of bananas from K18.95 to K29.06.

Across the 16 districts the centre monitors, the October basket averaged K7,660.84. Lusaka was the most expensive at K12,253.72, followed by Ndola at K10,449.46 and Solwezi at K9,646.87; Chinsali was the cheapest at K6,043.67, News Diggers and Zambia Monitor reported. The centre expects prices to keep rising while the El Niño-linked dry spell continues. It also noted that headline inflation has fallen to 6.3% in October from 11.2% in December 2025 without households feeling much relief.

The budget, read from the bottom

Much of the centre's statement was about the K284.8 billion 2027 budget presented on 6 November. The Daily Nation reported Fr Mutale's argument that the government should have imposed a windfall tax on high copper profits rather than add to the burden on citizens; that formal and semi-formal workers are taxed hardest because they are easy to find through the NAPSA, ZRA and NHIMA databases; that mining agreements should be negotiated while prices are favourable; and that debt service now exceeds spending on health, education, agriculture and social protection combined. Zambia Monitor reported the centre's specific objections: raising VAT from 16% to 16.5% and increasing the mobile-money levy will push up the price of essentials for low-income households, and the income-tax-free threshold stays at K5,100, where it has been since 2024, even as a new 39.5% rate applies to monthly income above K37,000.

The centre did find things to welcome, Zambia Monitor reported: the social cash transfer rises from K400 to K450 a month (K600 to K650 for households with a person with a disability), social protection spending goes from K15.6 billion to K16.2 billion and coverage from 1.5 million to more than 1.6 million households, disaster risk management funding rises from K81.4 million to K316.5 million, and 1,400 megawatts of non-hydro generation is planned. But K450 is 3.7% of the Lusaka basket, the centre pointed out, and there is no money for the Food Security Pack in 2027, which it called a major concern.

— What Fr Daniel Mutale, executive director of the JCTR, said a budget should do, in the centre's statement of Tuesday 10 November as quoted by Zambia Monitor

PricewaterhouseCoopers reached a similar conclusion from the other end of the income scale, News Diggers reported on Wednesday: Zambians will feel the budget through shopping, because the half-point VAT rise, the higher top PAYE rate and the mobile-money levy may each look small but add up at the till. The firm urged the government to set a clear, time-bound plan to settle K60 billion in outstanding VAT refunds. The budget targets K233.7 billion in domestic revenue, up from a K253 billion total budget in 2026.

The government's case for the tax measures was made at the budget symposium on Monday and in Parliament on Tuesday: domestic revenue must rise to 23.6% of GDP by 2029, Mr Nkulukusa said, and Zambia itself, in Dr Musokotwane's words to the House, is an example of a country that has become better off after an IMF programme, News Diggers reported.
