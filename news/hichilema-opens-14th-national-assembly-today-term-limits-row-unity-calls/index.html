Opening day: Hichilema addresses the 14th National Assembly this morning — with a term-limits row in the foyer and a divided country in the gallery

President Hakainde Hichilema ceremonially opens the First Session of the 14th National Assembly today, his first address to Parliament since re-election, expected to set out the Government's 2026–2027 policy direction on energy, development and free education. Outside the chamber, the week's argument has been about a clause he has not proposed — and about the reconciliation his critics say must come first.

By Chanda Mwape • LUSAKA — 9 October 2026 • 6 min read

The red carpet goes down this morning. President Hakainde Hichilema officially opens the First Session of the Fourteenth National Assembly at Parliament Buildings today, delivering the ceremonial address that sets the Government's policy direction for 2026–2027. It is his first speech to the House since the 13 August election returned him to office, and the first before an Assembly transformed by the constitutional reforms of his first term — up to 280 seats once the constituency, proportional-representation and nominated places are all filled, with Nelly Mutti re-elected unopposed as Speaker and Mufumbwe's Nshamba Muzungu installed as Second Deputy Speaker after a 166–58 vote.

State media previews say the Head of State has signalled an address built on the administration's load-bearing themes: energy, development and free education. The timing hands him a favourable week to cite — the United States health pact signed yesterday at the Ministry of Finance, installed power capacity at 4,722MW against peak demand of 2,400MW, and a World Bank upgrade that put Zambia among the region's improved economies. The Parliament he addresses is his party's house: the UPND holds the chamber comfortably, and the opposition benches are still arguing among themselves about who leads them.

Yet the week's loudest pre-speech argument has concerned a clause the President has never proposed. Kazungula North MP Dr Clement Andeleki declared that two presidential terms are "not enough", while former Mandevu lawmaker Christopher Shakafuswa warned against tailoring the Constitution around one man — the latest exchanges in a third-term debate that keeps resurfacing from inside the ruling party's own ranks. The constitutional text is not ambiguous: Article 106 bars a twice-elected president from contesting again, and changing it is a supermajority exercise. State House has repeatedly distanced itself from the talk. Today's podium offers the shortest possible route to ending it — one sentence, delivered to the nation, and the debate is dead. Silence, by contrast, will be read as strategy.

The other pressure on the address is older and sorer. The Evangelical Fellowship of Zambia's executive director, Allan Kasung'ami, urged the President this week to put national unity, reconciliation and the cost of living at the centre of the speech, saying the country remains polarised by politics. The historian Dr Chiputa went further, tying reconciliation directly to the opposition figures held on treason and sedition charges — Brian Mundubile and Makebi Zulu among them — arguing it is difficult to rally citizens behind the Grow Zambia Agenda while others are grieving or detained. Hichilema himself hinted at dialogue at the Isamu Lya Moomba ceremony in Bweengwa last Sunday. Whether that hint becomes a paragraph today is the second thing the gallery will be listening for.

Confirmed vs. alleged

Confirmed: The ceremonial opening and presidential address take place today at Parliament Buildings (National Assembly notice; order paper); Mutti's unopposed re-election and Muzungu's 166–58 victory on 2 October; Andeleki's "two terms not enough" remarks and Shakafuswa's warning (both on the record this week); the EFZ's public appeal; the constitutional bar in Article 106 as written.

Alleged or unverified: That any third-term plan exists beyond backbench remarks — repeatedly denied by State House and unproven; that the address will contain a reconciliation announcement or a term-limits line — unknown at press time; the speech's detailed content, which had not been delivered when this edition closed.
