September 30 is copper day: the export-duty decision that reveals Zambia's real bottleneck

Zambia extended its waiver on the 10% copper-concentrate export duty to the end of September while the country's biggest smelters sit in maintenance. What happens next is a test of industrial policy itself.

By Natasha Banda • 4 September 2026 • 6 min read

Zambia produced 890,346 tonnes of copper in 2025 — up about 8% but still short of the one-million-tonne target, and a fraction of the 3 million tonnes a year the government wants by 2031. With benchmark copper futures around US$14,000 a tonne, more than 40% higher than a year ago, the stakes of that ambition have never been larger.

The immediate test lands on 30 September, when the suspension of a 10% export duty on copper concentrates expires. The waiver — first imposed in August 2025 and now covering 271,742 tonnes of concentrate — exists because the country's biggest smelters are simultaneously down for maintenance: Mopani's Mufulira smelter faces an extended shutdown through mid-September, Chambishi is midway through a two-month outage, and Konkola's Nchanga smelter began a 60-day shutdown in May.

Duty-free concentrate quotas

  • Mopani: 100,000 t
  • Barrick Lumwana: 56,986 t
  • First Quantum & Nkana Mining: ~43,000 t each
  • Lubambe: 15,000 t · KCM: 12,541 t

More than US$10 billion has flowed into Zambian mining since 2021 — Barrick is spending US$2 billion to double Lumwana, First Quantum commissioned its US$1.25 billion Kansanshi S3 expansion, and KoBold's Mingomba project is priced at up to US$2.5 billion.

"A government can tax concentrate exports, but it can't tax a smelter into operating while that smelter is down for repairs."

Miners want the waiver extended; beneficiation advocates want the duty restored to force local processing. The decision will signal whether industrial policy and industrial capacity have finally been sequenced — or whether ambition keeps outrunning the smelters.
