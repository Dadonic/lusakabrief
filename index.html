Twenty-four ministers in an afternoon: Hichilema fills the 30-ministry Cabinet, gives Tambatamba the mines and splits Energy between Kapala and Chikote

A day after Parliament approved the new structure, President Hakainde Hichilema swore in 24 Cabinet ministers at State House on Wednesday 28 October, filling the posts left empty since August. Brenda Tambatamba moves from Labour to Mines, Peter Kapala and Makozo Chikote take the new Electricity and Petroleum ministries, Felix Mutati and Douglas Syakalima return, and Jack Mwiimbu moves from Home Affairs to Justice. Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

What the names say: continuity at the centre, new owners for the growth targets

Read as a whole, Wednesday's list is conservative where the state is coercive and experimental where it is productive. Defence, Justice, Home Affairs and Local Government went to first-term ministers moved sideways or returned, and the two nominated MPs sworn in on Tuesday were in Cabinet the next day. The new ministries, by contrast, went to a mix: two former energy ministers split Energy between them, a civil-society policy specialist takes Trade and Exports, an economist from the road fund takes Industry, and a former PF MP takes Culture and Arts. That pattern supports the government's account of the realignment, that each Grow Zambia target now has a named owner, and it also supports the critics' point, that the test is whether owners with new letterheads can deliver what one ministry could not. Two things remain open. The published documents still give no cost, and the first budget these ministers defend is now a week away. And the outlets disagree on who holds Education and who holds Skills and Higher Education, which is the kind of detail a gazette settles; until it is gazetted, the lists are reports.

Analysis

A budget delayed a week is still a budget written before the Cabinet existed

Moving the reading to 6 November answers the narrowest form of Citizens First's objection: ministers appointed on Wednesday will not be asked to defend estimates on Friday. It does not answer the wider one, that the 2027 budget was built by the Treasury and the four ministers who existed while the other 24 posts were empty, and that the new ministers inherit it rather than shape it. The government's reply, implicit in the sequence of the last week, is that the realignment had to come first precisely so that the estimates could be read against the ministries that will spend them. What the week buys is time to redraw the votes along the new lines, and that is worth watching: thirty ministries need thirty heads of expenditure. The substance is unchanged by the date. ZIPAR wants PAYE left alone and the revenue base widened; the President told his ministers that PAYE alone would lift revenue and that investors could be given incentives; farmers are owed K14.6 billion that the supplementary budget is meant to clear.

Analysis

Three sittings, no hearing: what the prison's absence does to the bail application

The application before the High Court is about delay: the 18 argue that holding them while the State continues to investigate is itself the violation. Each adjournment therefore does something an ordinary adjournment does not. It extends the detention the applicants say is unlawful, and it adds to the record the court will weigh. Wednesday's order, that the prison produce the 18 at 10:00 and that its officer in charge explain any failure in person, is the court saying so. The State's position is a reasonable one on its face: it filed its affidavit on time and the defence asked for more time to reply. The defence's position is also reasonable: it could not finalise a reply its clients had not signed because the prison did not bring them. What neither side controls is the Correctional Service, and the question the court may now have to decide first is why a directive to produce prisoners was not followed. Whatever the panel rules on bail, the charges of treason, terrorism and sedition remain untried, and the 18 are presumed innocent of all of them. Thursday morning will show whether the hearing finally begins.
