Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

A statement that declines to settle the one question being asked

State House's Sunday statement is carefully built. It does not say the President wants a third term, and it does not say he would refuse one. It says the debate belongs to citizens and that he will not be drawn. Supporters of the government can fairly point out that the executive has no formal role in a petition to Parliament, that a presidential intervention either way could be read as pressure on MPs, and that Justice Minister Jack Mwiimbu has already said the Assembly is the right place for the petitions. Opponents can fairly reply that the question is not procedural but personal: the only person who can rule out benefiting from a change to the two-term limit is the man it would benefit, and Given Lubinda's challenge was framed exactly that way. Cosmas Musumali's charge of indirect endorsement rests on the same gap. What to watch is not State House but the National Assembly: whether the three petitions are tabled, which committee takes them, and whether any MP sponsors a bill. Until then the row runs on silence, and silence can be read as whichever side prefers.

Analysis

Mpika's gold: the State is formalising with one hand and arresting with the other

The weekend's arrests at Muneshi Stream arrived four days after regulators sat down with Chief Chikwanda and local miners to explain how artisanal gold rights will be allocated at Kamambwe, Kanyelele and Danger Hill. The two moves are meant to work together: tenements for Zambian citizens who register, enforcement against those who dig and process without licences. The Commission's account of a site suspended two months ago and back in operation shows why the second half matters, and the suspected pollution of a stream used by villages downstream explains the involvement of ZEMA. What is still unknown is what happens next. No charges have been reported against the 20 arrested, no equipment or gold has been listed as seized, and the Immigration Department's separate action, a deportation after a shooting and 15 people held over permits, concerns different sites and different offences. Mercury use, which the provincial administration raised at the Mpika meeting, has not been confirmed at Muneshi. The test of the formalisation drive will be whether licensed Zambian miners are working these sites by the next rainy season, and whether the courts, rather than press statements, decide what the 20 did.

Analysis

Half the normal rain in Lusaka, and a season that may be 90 days long

The Met Department's first bulletin confirms what the El Niño forecast implied: a split country. Northern and Luapula have had more rain than usual and may plant now; Lusaka, Southern and Central have had about half their normal total and are told to wait, with a season that could last 90 days or less. The practical advice is specific and demanding. Drought-tolerant crops, maize on a small area in extra-early varieties, planting in two or three batches a week apart, and no replanting after the false start of early October until a district is marked Plant. For smallholders who have already bought maize seed, switching part of a field to sorghum or cowpeas is not a free choice, and the advice to plant in batches assumes labour and seed to spare. For the government, which has named food security as a priority, a poor southern harvest in 2027 would be a budget problem as much as a farming one. The counter-argument is that forecasts are probabilities, that the northern half of the country has had above-average rain, and that a short season well managed can still produce a crop. What to watch is the next bulletin, due at the end of November, and whether the Copperbelt's planting window around 20 to 26 November actually opens.
