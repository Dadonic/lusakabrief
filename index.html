Three petitions and no sponsor: Mwiimbu says the government will not interfere with calls to remove term limits, and will not say whether Hichilema would sign one

Justice Minister Jack Mwiimbu told a Lusaka press briefing on Friday 13 November that three petitions asking Parliament to amend Article 106(3), the two-term limit, were initiated by citizens and are not sponsored by President Hichilema, the UPND or the government, which will leave the National Assembly to handle them. Asked whether the President would back an amendment if MPs passed one, he said he could not speak for him. UPND legal chairperson Mulambo Haimbe says the party has never discussed the matter.

Health · NHIMA

Two per cent of everything: Cabinet moves the NHIMA deduction from basic to gross pay from January 2027, as the Treasury takes on the scheme's K1.7 billion debt and dialysis bill

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

Three petitions move the question from who proposed this to what Parliament does

Until Friday the term-limit argument had a proposal without a document: an idea attributed to a nominated MP, a joint statement from ten defeated candidates against it, and a civil society threat of litigation. The Justice Minister's briefing supplies the missing paper. Three petitions on Article 106(3) are now before the National Assembly, and the executive says it will neither sponsor nor block them. That is a defensible constitutional position: petitioning is a right under Article 88(1), and the Assembly is the right body to receive it. It is also politically convenient, because it leaves the President's own view unstated, which is exactly what Mwiimbu declined to supply. The strongest case for the government is that it has proposed nothing and cannot be asked to oppose what citizens are entitled to raise. The strongest case for its critics is that silence from State House is itself a signal when the ruling party's own members have floated the idea. What to watch: whether the Clerk publishes the petitions and names the petitioners; whether the relevant committee schedules hearings; and whether the President, or the UPND through Haimbe's office, answers the direct question his minister would not.

Analysis

Gross pay widens NHIMA's base; the State taking the debt is the bigger admission

Moving the 2% deduction from basic to gross pay is the visible part of Cabinet's NHIMA decision, and it is the part workers will notice in January. Where allowances form a large share of a pay packet, the same percentage will yield materially more. But the minister's own framing matters more: he said the scheme was set up on flawed financial grounds and could not meet its obligations. Taking K1.7 billion of supplier debt onto the Treasury, paying dialysis directly, and buying cover for elderly non-contributors and cash-transfer beneficiaries amounts to the State acknowledging that an insurance fund cannot carry universal obligations on a contributory base alone. The case for the package is that providers who have gone unpaid get settled and the fund restarts clean. The case against, from the wage-earner's side, is that it arrives in the same budget as a VAT rise to 16.5%, and that no change to the employer's share has been reported. Unknown so far: the legal instrument that will give effect to the change, how allowances in kind will be treated, and whether NHIMA's benefit package expands to match the larger contribution.

Analysis

Four points from three is not a crisis, but Bujumbura now carries the weight of the group

A home draw with the side at the bottom of the group is a poor result, and Lwandamina said as much. The arithmetic is less bleak than the mood. Algeria's win in Tizi Ouzou puts them on seven points after three rounds and leaves Zambia on four, Togo on three and Burundi on two, so Zambia remain second and the sides below them are within a point or two. The concern is form rather than position: the coach himself said his players failed to reproduce their training, made basic errors in ball control and conceded from a single mistake once the midfield had settled, and both ZamFoot and News Diggers describe a first half in which Burundi had the better chances. The case for patience is that the substitutions were forced by fatigue and injury, not design, and that the squad is already in Bujumbura with three days to prepare. What to watch on Tuesday: whether Chilufya starts after being withdrawn, whether Mwanza's early saves were the exception or the pattern, and whether a point in Bujumbura is treated as acceptable or as another drop, with Togo hosting Algeria the same day.
