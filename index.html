Economy · Lead story

One notch up, three days before the budget: Moody's lifts Zambia from Caa2 to Caa1 with a positive outlook, and the Treasury says the rating is a means, not an end

Moody's Ratings raised Zambia's sovereign credit rating to Caa1 on Friday 30 October and kept the outlook positive, the third agency to move the country up since the 2020 default. The Ministry of Finance and National Planning welcomed the decision on Monday 2 November, crediting debt restructuring, fiscal consolidation and a steadier economy, and warning that the rating must be turned into investment and jobs. Situmbeko Musokotwane presents the 2027 budget on Friday 6 November, a week later than first planned.

Freddie Mwila, the captain of 1967, the Atlanta Chief who scored twice against Manchester City, and the coach who won Zambia's only continental club title, dies at 80

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

What a one-notch upgrade buys, and what Friday's budget has to show for it

Caa1 is still a rating that tells lenders to expect trouble, so the practical value of Friday's upgrade is not cheaper money tomorrow but direction. Three agencies have now moved Zambia the same way since the 2020 default, the IMF staff have agreed a new programme, and Moody's has kept a positive outlook, which is a promise to look again if the trend holds. The test of whether it holds is the document Situmbeko Musokotwane reads out on Friday 6 November. Investors will look for three things: a deficit path consistent with the restructured debt, an explanation of how 30 ministries fit a consolidating budget, and a plan for revenue that does not depend on copper staying high. The Ministry's own caution, that a rating is a means rather than an end, is the right frame; the counter-argument, made by Stephen Kampyongo among others, is that a budget delivered a week late and followed by a ten-day suspension leaves Parliament little time to test any of it. Still unknown: the full text of the Moody's reasoning, and whether the Board approves the IMF facility before year-end.

Analysis

A seat without a vote: what Mandevu settles and what it leaves open

On Thursday Adrian Banda becomes MP for Mandevu without a ballot being cast, and the law on that is not in dispute: the Electoral Commission has cited the regulations, the withdrawal letters exist, and one valid candidate means a declaration. Everything around it is contested. The Tonse Alliance says its candidate was threatened, names a provincial minister and has played a recording; the UPND says the claim is malicious and unfounded and that nobody was pushed. Neither account has been tested anywhere that can test it. The Commission's notice gave no reasons for the withdrawals and did not say whether it had received a complaint, and the Inspector General's security plan for Mandevu was drawn up for a contest that will not happen. Three things to watch: whether the alliance's promised complaint to the police is lodged and what becomes of it; whether the Commission says anything about the allegations once the declaration is made; and whether the pattern of late withdrawals repeats in the next round of by-elections. Thursday's ward polls, including Mbeta in Sioma, will be the first measure.

Analysis

The K5 argument: two explanations for the pump price that do not exclude each other

State House and the oil marketers are describing the same price from different ends of the supply chain. The government's account is global: Brent near US$112, tankers under attack, and a tax holiday that cost US$200 million and could not be extended without cutting into free education and cash transfers. The marketers' account is domestic: a pipeline whose open-access rules, they say, let a few companies earn about K5 a litre more than competition would allow. Both can be true at once, which is why the row matters. If the marketers are right, there is a saving available that does not require the Treasury to forgo revenue; if the government is right about its fiscal limits, the pipeline is one of the few levers left. What is not yet known is whether the Ministry of Petroleum's review of the monthly pricing cycle will also look at the Open Access Framework, and what the K5 figure rests on, since the association's detailed argument sits behind a paywall and no response from the ERB has been reported. The December return of VAT, in phases, is the next scheduled pressure.
