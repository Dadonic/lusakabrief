Another week in Mukobeko: the State opposes bail for Mundubile and 17 others — and the hearing moves to 28 October with the DPP joined, the Zambian Observer reports

Brian Mundubile, Makebi Zulu and 16 co-accused came back to the Lusaka High Court on Wednesday 21 October for the constitutional bail hearing that was adjourned on Monday. The State has now filed an affidavit opposing release, arguing that treason cases take time and that the Director of Public Prosecutions must be a party. The Zambian Observer reports that both sides agreed to join Gilbert Phiri and that the hearing now goes to 28 October. The 18 remain in custody in Kabwe.

Analysis

What joining the DPP to the bail case changes — and what it does not

The State’s argument that a constitutional bail application is criminal in nature, and so cannot be decided without the Director of Public Prosecutions, has now been accepted by consent, if the Zambian Observer’s account of Wednesday’s hearing holds. For the applicants that costs another week in Kabwe; for the State it means the prosecutor who said on 14 September that treason investigations were continuing must now answer, on affidavit, why 18 people should stay in custody while they continue. That is the question the panel has been asked to decide, and the affidavit already filed gives the State’s answer: treason cases are long, the charges are grave, and the court should weigh flight risk and public safety. The applicants’ answer is that the Constitution guarantees a court appearance without undue delay, whatever the charge. Two things remain outside the case. The joinder does not bring forward any trial date, and nothing in the bail ruling will say whether the militia allegations are true. Watch 28 October for the DPP’s affidavit, and for whether the court rules from the bench or reserves.

Analysis

The Speaker chose the secretary general’s letter. The caucus still has to live with it

Speaker Mutti’s route out of the opposition leadership dispute was procedural: she took the party’s formal communication, signed by its secretary general, as decisive. That avoids the harder question of which meeting — 3 September at Palmwood Lodge or 27 September at another Lusaka lodge — better reflected the will of NRPUP MPs, and it leaves Article 74(2) untested, since the Constitution says the largest opposition party elects its leader but not how the House verifies the election. The strongest case for the ruling is that a Speaker cannot referee internal party contests and must rely on the party’s own organs. The strongest case against it, from the group that backed Stephen Kampyongo, is that a party president who is also the parliamentary leader concentrates power that the caucus had tried to share. What follows depends on numbers nobody has published: how many MPs attended the second meeting and whether they now accept the Whip’s instructions. The first contested vote in the House will show whether the opposition speaks with one voice or two.

Analysis

K626 million uncollected is not theft — which is why it keeps happening

Audit reports attract attention for missing money, but the 2025 report’s biggest numbers are revenue nobody chased and bills nobody paid. The Ministry of Mines did not collect K626.64 million in penalties from licence holders in the same year the President was telling the Mining Insaka that tenements must be explored or surrendered; a penalty that is assessed but not enforced is a weaker signal than one that is. The K3.56 billion owed by the Ministry of Health is a different problem: arrears that go back to 2014 cannot be fixed by a single budget line, and every year they roll forward they attract interest and suppliers’ reluctance. The fall in unresolved findings from 33 to 27 is real progress, but it measures paperwork closed, not kwacha recovered. The next step is parliamentary: the Public Accounts Committee will call controlling officers, and the Treasury will decide whether to clear the health arrears or let them ride. Watch for whether the mining penalties are collected or written off, and whether the 5,568 missing mukula logs produce a prosecution.
