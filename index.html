Economy · Agriculture

Nearly three in four farmers have their inputs, and some are selling them: the ministry warns against resale and shared codes as the budget plans a move from FISP to farm loans

As of Friday 6 November, 742,447 farmers, or 72.47% of the 1,024,434 targeted, had redeemed their inputs under the 2026/27 Farmer Input Support Programme, the Ministry of Agriculture said on Saturday. It also said it had received reports from several districts of farmers selling redeemed inputs to agro-dealers and sharing their e-voucher codes with cooperative leaders, and warned both farmers and dealers of action. The 2027 budget gives FISP K9.1 billion within K22.7 billion for agriculture.

Economy · Lead story

K284.8 billion and half a point on VAT: Musokotwane's 2027 budget taxes mining dividends at 15%, adds a 39.5% PAYE band and aims the deficit at 3% of GDP

The Minister of Finance and National Planning, Situmbeko Musokotwane, presented a K284.8 billion budget for 2027 to the National Assembly on Friday 6 November, the largest in Zambia's history and 28.4% of GDP. Domestic revenue is to cover K233.7 billion of it, borrowing K41.9 billion and grants K9.2 billion. VAT rises from 16% to 16.5%, a 15% withholding tax lands on mining company dividends, a new 39.5% PAYE band applies to monthly pay above K37,000, and debt service alone takes K67.6 billion.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

The mobile money levy: easy to collect, hard to defend, and still not itemised

Of all the measures in Friday's budget, the rise in mobile money levies is the one most Zambians will feel first, and it is the one whose details are least visible. The government's case, as reported, is that the levy broadens the tax base, improves equity and raises revenue from a channel through which a great deal of money now moves outside the banks. The industry's case is that the levy is a charge on the poorest users of the financial system, that it raises the cost of every small transfer and that it risks pushing people back to cash, undoing the inclusion gains the government itself celebrates. Both can be true at once. What is missing is the arithmetic: none of the reports read on Saturday gave the new rates or bands, and one outlet's description of the levy as doubled has not been confirmed elsewhere. Two things to watch: the text of the revenue bills when they reach Parliament's committees, and whether the Ministry of Finance publishes a band-by-band table. Until it does, both the association's warning and the government's reassurance are arguments about a number nobody outside the ministry has seen.

Analysis

A march about a bill that does not exist: what 25 November actually tests

The government's objection to the 25 November procession is that there is nothing to march against: no bill, no party resolution, no presidential statement. The organisers' answer is that a petition to abolish term limits was received by the National Assembly on 27 October, that its supporters were allowed to walk to Parliament, and that the only way to show the other view exists is to walk too. Both positions are defensible, and the dispute is less about term limits than about who may gather in public and when. That is why the first real test comes before the march. The coalition has given notice to the police; whether the police acknowledge it, impose conditions or invoke the Public Order Act will say more about the state of assembly rights than any speech on the day. The second test is inside the ruling party. A UPND member's widely shared plea to shut the debate down shows the question is not simply government against civil society. What to watch: the police response to the notice, any statement from the UPND's own organs, and whether the 27 October petition is ever tabled as a motion.

Analysis

Tuesday's ruling is about jurisdiction, not liberty, and that is the point

When the Lusaka High Court rules on Tuesday 10 November, it will not be deciding whether 18 people accused of treason should be released. It will be deciding whether it can hear the question at all. The State's preliminary objection, raised on Thursday, is that the constitutional bail application is wrongly before the court; the defence's reply is that the objection is a device to keep the application from being argued, and the defence has also argued that the prosecution's own manual contemplates constitutional bail where a trial is delayed. If the objection is sustained, the application falls away and the applicants' next step has not been reported. If it is overruled, the defence says the substantive hearing starts at once, with the State's readiness then the issue. Either way, the applicants remain at Mukobeko with no trial date set, since the court said in October that dates would follow its ruling on bail, and the Solwezi court's decision to send the separate sedition case to Lusaka adds a second file to the same calendar. What to watch: the ruling itself, the reasons given for it, and whether any trial date follows.
