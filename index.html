Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

Weight or admissibility: why Friday's ruling matters beyond one flash disk

The State's second objection looks procedural, but the question underneath it is one the High Court will have to answer for every constitutional application that follows. The State says a video on a flash disk must be authenticated under Section 9 of the Electronic Communications and Transactions Act before a court may look at it, and that an affidavit in reply cannot introduce new material. The applicants say a flash disk is a document under the Evidence Act, that an affidavit sworn before a Commissioner for Oaths is properly before the court, and that doubts about a recording's provenance go to how much weight it carries, not whether it can be seen. If the panel agrees with the State, the bail application will be argued without the footage the defence says explains why the accused went to the United Nations rather than a police station. If it agrees with the applicants, the State's flight-risk argument will be answered on the record by a video it has not been able to examine. Either way, Friday's ruling is the first time this court will say how electronic evidence enters a constitutional bail hearing. The 18 have now been in custody for nearly three months without that hearing starting; that fact does not change whichever way the ruling goes.

Analysis

Two readings of the same budget: the Treasury's arithmetic and the JCTR's basket

The Finance Minister's debt statement and the Jesuit centre's cost-of-living report describe the same economy from opposite ends. The Treasury's story is of a balance sheet repaired: external debt down 5% in six months, US$275 million saved on a 2053 bond, a new IMF programme at staff level and domestic revenue heading for 23.6% of GDP. The JCTR's is of a Lusaka household that needed K854 more in October than in September just to stand still, and faces half a point more VAT and a higher mobile-money levy under the budget proposals. Both are true; the dispute is over sequencing. The government's case, as the minister set it out on Sunday and Tuesday, is that years of donor support are over and services must be paid for from domestic taxes; inflation's fall from 11.2% to 6.3% is the backdrop it points to. The centre's and PwC's case is that the people easiest to tax are formal workers and small traders, that a windfall levy on copper would have spread the load, and that K60 billion in unpaid VAT refunds is itself a tax on business. What to watch: the IMF board in December, whether the Food Security Pack reappears in the estimates, and the November basket, due in early December, which will show whether the dry-season spike in vegetables was a blip or a trend.

Analysis

El Niño is a 2029 problem being managed with 2026 tools

ZESCO's plan to reach 11,630 megawatts by 2030 with hydro at a third of the mix is the right answer to the wrong year. The dry spell forecasters expect this rainy season would show in the reservoirs in 2027, before most of that solar and thermal capacity exists and before the line to Tanzania opens in 2029. What the utility can do now is what it did in late 2025, when new solar, thermal and independent projects let it end nationwide load shedding in December: keep every new plant feeding the grid and buy in what it cannot generate. Nkana Water's 18% cut at Bulangililo shows how early the water side of a drought arrives; the Kafue at Kitwe is falling in November, not March. The minister's push on the REA is about something different, connections for people who have already paid, but it points at the same constraint: a grid can be expanded on paper faster than it can be built. Two things will tell whether this season is managed better than 2024. First, whether ZESCO publishes a contingency schedule before the rains fail rather than after. Second, how much of the 2030 capacity reaches financial close in the next twelve months. The targets are credible; the calendar is the problem.
