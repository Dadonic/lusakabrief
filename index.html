Opinion

Publish the text, then take the bow

If today's signatures land as advertised, the Government will have earned a genuine negotiating win: the specimen clause is dead, the data-sharing is narrowed to monitoring, and the minister can say 'no mention of critical minerals' on the record. But a win described is not a win delivered. The sceptics — M'membe on the left, the DPP's Mwanza on the flank — are asking for one thing that costs the Government nothing: the text. Publish it, in full, and let every clause answer for itself. And a second request that costs rather more: the US$2.1 billion Zambia has pledged from its own side must actually appear in the health budget, year after year, or this afternoon's ceremony becomes a photograph of a promise. Zimbabwe refused this architecture and lost US$367 million; Zambia negotiated it and kept the money. The difference is called leverage — and leverage, once used, must be defended in print.

Opinion

An upgrade is not an outcome

The World Bank has moved Zambia into its column of upgraded economies, and the Treasury is entitled to a measured smile: reforms it defended through two difficult years are being acknowledged by the institution that grades them. But read the same report to the end and the smile should fade. Median inflation across the region is climbing back toward 5.5 percent; income per person barely moves; the absolute number of poor Africans still rises. An upgrade is a forecast about the economy; it is not a fact about a household. The test of this week's good headline arrives in the mundane places — the price of mealie meal, the queue at the clinic, the megawatts that actually reach a mine. The Bank itself says the next challenge is turning growth into jobs. That sentence should be stapled to every speech given in Lusaka this month, starting with tomorrow's.

Opinion

The forgery that found its audience

A fake circular banning church services until November did not spread this week because Zambians are gullible; it spread because it was plausible. Three weeks into a cholera outbreak, with buses being impounded and pavements cleared in the name of public health, 'government suspends gatherings' sounds less like mischief and more like Tuesday. That is the real lesson for both the ministry and the media houses it scolded: disinformation does not invent fear, it rents it. The permanent secretary is right that the forgers should be found and charged. But the durable defence is boring and institutional — publish real directives promptly, on official channels, in predictable formats, so that the anonymous PDF has nowhere to hide. In an information emergency, the fastest credible voice wins. Government should plan to be it, not merely to condemn whoever beat it to the phone screens.
