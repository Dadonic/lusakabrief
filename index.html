Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

An Urgent Action changes the audience, not the law

Amnesty International's appeal does not bind anyone. Urgent Actions are letters to a government and a call to members to write their own; their force is reputational, and governments routinely answer that the matter is before the courts. That answer is available here, because it is true: the High Court rules on Friday on whether the State's preliminary objection blocks the sedition bail application, and on Tuesday on constitutional bail for all 18. What the Amnesty document does is widen the audience for those two rulings, from Lusaka to the diplomatic missions and lenders who read Amnesty's index numbers. It also fixes a version of the August timeline on the record, including allegations of torture and of a death during the 14 August raid, which the State has not publicly answered. The strongest case for the government is the one Elvis Nkandu made: it does not control judges, and the charges will be tested in court. The strongest case against it is Amnesty's: 16 days without charge is not a matter for a judge to cure later. What to watch is less the rulings themselves than their reasoning, and whether any ministry replies to UA 121/26 at all.

Analysis

The 2014 judgment gives the consortium a procedure, not a march

The anti-third-term consortium has chosen the one line of attack the police statement left open. LAZ v The Attorney General, decided in 2014, is read by the organisers as requiring the police, when they judge a procession unsafe, to say why and to offer another date. Tuesday's statement did neither: it cited intelligence it did not describe and proposed no alternative. If no date arrives by Thursday the groups say they will appeal to the Home Affairs Minister, who has already said he expects nobody to march because there is no third-term bid to march against. That sets up the next question: whether the matter goes to court before 25 November, and whether a court will order the police to do what the consortium says the 2014 ruling requires. The government's position is coherent on its own terms: the President is not a candidate, petitions are a citizen's right, and the police are the judge of security. The organisers' position is equally coherent: the right to assemble is not conditional on the cause being necessary. Neither side has yet said what happens if people walk anyway.

Analysis

Musokotwane's two defences are really one argument

In Kitwe the finance minister argued for foreign investors; in the same news cycle he argued for the IMF. They are the same case made to two different audiences. The Copperbelt hears the investor argument as a defence of the mining houses whose expansions carry the 930,000-tonne target for 2027 and the three-million-tonne ambition for 2031. The national audience hears the IMF argument as a defence of a second programme, US$1.472 billion over 36 months, agreed at staff level in October and still awaiting the board. What links them is the minister's view that Zambia lacks capital and should borrow credibility where it can, citing China, Japan and Germany on one hand and Saudi Arabia's seat at the IMF's top table on the other. The counter-arguments are familiar and not frivolous: that investor-friendly terms have in the past cost the Treasury revenue, and that Fund programmes carry conditions on spending and tax whose weight tends to fall on households before it falls on mines. The test will be the board date for the new arrangement and whether the 2027 budget's mining tax promises survive the first copper price dip.
