Politics · Lead story

'Zambia does not belong to an individual': the ten candidates Hichilema beat in August sign one statement against removing presidential term limits

Harry Kalaba, Fred M'membe, Kelvin Bwalya Fube and seven other candidates from the 13 August election issued a joint statement in Lusaka on Thursday 12 November rejecting a proposal, attributed to the nominated youth MP Mwato Kabaso, to abolish presidential term limits. They cite the one-party years, Frederick Chiluba's failed third-term bid in 2001 and the 2024 ruling on Edgar Lungu's eligibility, and call on MPs of every party to defend the two-term rule. Zambia Monitor, the Daily Nation and the Zambian Observer carried the statement.

US$30.89 billion owed, US$275 million saved: external debt fell 5% after the Eurobond buyback, Musokotwane tells Parliament, as the IMF board weighs a US$1.46 billion programme

Central government debt stood at US$30.89 billion at the end of June 2026, split almost evenly between domestic and external borrowing, the Finance Minister told the National Assembly on Tuesday 10 November. Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

A statement against a proposal that does not yet exist on paper

The ten candidates have taken a position before there is anything formal to oppose. All three outlets attribute the term-limit idea to a nominated youth MP, and none reports a bill, a motion or a government endorsement. That timing is the point: the signatories want the two-term rule defended as a principle before any text appears, so that a future proposal arrives into a settled argument rather than an open one. The strongest case for their side is history, which they make themselves: the one-party years and the 2001 third-term fight. The strongest case on the other side, which the statement acknowledges and tries to answer, is cost and continuity, the argument that frequent elections are expensive and that a term limit can remove a leader voters still want. What is unknown is whether anyone in the governing party intends to carry the idea further. The things to watch are a response from Kabaso or from State House, any notice of a constitutional amendment bill, and whether UPND MPs answer the statement's direct appeal to them.

Analysis

Why the first week of maize payments matters more than the total

The K14.7 billion for farmers is three-quarters of the supplementary budget Parliament approved, and the government has now attached a date to it. The number that will decide how the policy is judged, though, is not the total but the pace. Farmers delivered maize to the FRA months ago on a promise of payment, and the inputs for the coming season are being bought now. For any farmer who financed the last season on credit, a fortnight's difference in when the money lands changes whether it goes into the ground or into servicing debt. The Treasury's side of the argument is that it had to find the money through a supplementary budget, and that releasing K14.7 billion in days rather than weeks has consequences for liquidity and for the kwacha. What remains unknown is the disbursement schedule: how many farmers are paid on Friday, in which provinces, and whether the oldest deliveries are settled first. If the payments run smoothly, the political cost of the delay fades. If they stall again, Saviour Chishimba's demand for an apology will be the mildest reaction.

Analysis

Eleven o'clock: what the High Court's ruling on five paragraphs will settle

The objection the three-judge panel rules on this morning looks small. The State wants five paragraphs and a video removed from the affidavit of the 18 applicants; the defence says the material explains why two of them took refuge at a UN facility and answers the flight-risk argument the State itself raised. Underneath is a question that will shape every constitutional bail application after this one: whether an applicant may answer the State's objections in a reply affidavit, or must anticipate them in the original filing. The State's position, put by Chief State Advocate Chali Hambayi, is procedural fairness, that it cannot respond to matters pleaded late. The defence's, put by Milner Katolo, is that a clarification is not new evidence and that the court should weigh it rather than exclude it. Either way, the substantive bail hearing follows the ruling, and the sedition plea in the magistrates' court is set for 24 November. What is unknown is whether Mundubile and Zulu will have new lawyers by then, and whether the High Court's reasoning on the affidavit will be applied to the sedition file too.
