Courts · Lead story Developing

‘No jurisdiction’: Solwezi sends the Mundubile and Zulu sedition case to the High Court — and the question of a Lusaka hearing is left for the judges

Principal Resident Magistrate Mambwe Mukonde ruled on Tuesday 13 October that the Solwezi Magistrates’ Court cannot hear the two-count sedition case against Brian Mundubile and Makebi Zulu, and referred it to the High Court. The State had not disputed the defence’s claim that the alleged offences took place in Lusaka. No High Court date has been set, the two men have still not taken plea, and the separate treason matter is untouched. In Lusaka, a bench warrant was issued for Binwell Mpundu in his own sedition case.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

What Solwezi decided — and what it left open

Tuesday’s ruling is narrower than either side’s supporters may read it. Magistrate Mukonde did not find the charges weak or the prosecution improper; she found that a Subordinate Court in Solwezi was not the forum, because the State never contested that the alleged acts happened in Lusaka. The defence won the point it argued, and the case now moves to the High Court. But she also declined to say where that court should sit, so the Lusaka question is still live. The State resisted the challenge; it may now argue the question of venue afresh. For the accused, the practical effect is weeks, not days: no High Court date exists, plea has still not been taken, and the treason detention runs on regardless. What to watch: the first High Court listing; whether the DPP seeks to keep the matter outside Lusaka; and how the detention question is handled alongside the sedition case.

Analysis

Falling debt, rising interest: two Moody’s numbers that are both true

State House led with the flattering number, and it is real: Moody’s expects Zambia’s debt stock to fall faster than almost any in sub-Saharan Africa. The uncomfortable number sits in the same report. About 35 percent of government revenue will go on interest in 2027 — the heaviest burden in the region, shared with Kenya. Both follow from the same history. The restructuring reduced the stock of debt; the cost of what remains, and of the domestic borrowing that fills the gap, is what keeps the interest bill high. The Government’s case is that discipline under the new IMF programme brings the second number down behind the first. The sceptics’ case is that a Treasury already telling the FRA to expect no money next season has little room if interest stays that high. What to watch in Bangkok: whether the ‘prior actions’ for the US$1.47 billion facility are published, and what the commercial lenders on the delegation’s list are offered.

Analysis

A seat in Geneva, earned without a vote

Zambia’s election to the Human Rights Council was a diplomatic success, and the Government is entitled to say so. It is also the second year in which every regional group filled its seats without a contest, which is why Human Rights Watch used the word ‘mockery’. Both things can be held at once. The case for the seat, as Lusaka made it, is a record of reform — the abolition of the death penalty, disability and child protection — and a promise to bring developing-country concerns to a body critics say is remote. The case against is not aimed at Zambia alone: that uncontested slates let any state through, and that membership now sits beside treason and sedition prosecutions of opposition figures that the Council’s own mechanisms may one day examine. A seat brings scrutiny with it. What to watch: how Zambia votes on country resolutions in its first session, and whether its envoys welcome or resist attention to the cases at home.
