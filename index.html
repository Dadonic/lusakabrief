Copper's worst day since April 2025: a fall of nearly 6 per cent on Wednesday wipes out the metal's November gains and leaves the price close to where the year began

Copper lost close to 6 per cent on Wednesday 18 November, its steepest one-day fall since April 2025, before steadying on Thursday, according to bank commentary carried by Zambia Monitor and an analysis by the broker XTB. The metal is down roughly 12 to 13 per cent in November and trades near US$12,560 a tonne, about where it started 2026. For Zambia, whose kwacha slipped to just under K20.50 to the dollar last week, the question is whether September's record highs were the peak.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

A copper correction is a budget question before it is a currency one

A 6 per cent day in copper is loud, but the number that matters for Zambia is quieter: where the price settles over the next quarter. Zambia's revenue plans were drawn up while copper was near the record highs it reached in September, and the kwacha's firmness this year has leaned on the metal. If Thursday's stabilisation holds and the price sits near US$12,500 a tonne, that is roughly where the year began, not a crisis. If XTB's next support near US$11,680 gives way, the arithmetic changes for royalties, which rise and fall with the price, and for the export receipts that supply the interbank market. Two things are worth separating. The first is the financial market, which is unwinding a bet on artificial intelligence and tariffs. The second is the physical market, where both commentaries say Chinese stocks are critically low and spot copper trades at a premium. The first can fall a long way without the second moving. What to watch: Friday's London close, any Bank of Zambia comment on the kwacha, and whether the Treasury's revenue assumptions for 2027 are revisited in Parliament's budget debate.

Analysis

Today's ruling is about the door, not the room

Whatever Judge Geoffrey Mulenga decides at 09:30, nobody walks out of Mukobeko today. The question before him is narrow: whether the Lusaka High Court can hear a bail application in a sedition case that began in Solwezi and has since been transferred to the Lusaka Subordinate Court. The State's case is procedural and not trivial. Bail is ordinarily sought first where the case is, and the DPP argues that going straight to the High Court while the matter sat in another court duplicated proceedings. The defence answers that no bail application was ever made in Solwezi, so there is nothing to duplicate, and that section 123 of the Criminal Procedure Code gives the High Court the power it needs. If the objection is upheld, the two men start again before a magistrate; if it is dismissed, the bail hearing itself proceeds, and the State can still oppose bail on the merits. Either way, the larger decision comes on Tuesday, when three judges rule on constitutional bail for all 18 treason accused, a charge the law ordinarily treats as non-bailable. The reasoning in both rulings will be read closely abroad, after Amnesty International's appeal this week.

Analysis

The term-limits fight is moving from slogans to procedure

For a fortnight the argument over presidential term limits was conducted in statements. This week it shifted to rules, which is usually where constitutional disputes are decided. Three procedural questions are now open. First, the police: having advised two consortiums not to march on 25 November, they have been asked in writing for specific reasons and an alternative, with a hint of litigation if none comes. Public-order law in Zambia has been tested in court before, and a bare citation of intelligence may not satisfy a judge. Second, the Speaker: William Harrington's argument that Nelly Mutti should not preside, because she sat on the Mung'omba commission that rejected a third term, is a recusal claim of a kind Parliament rarely entertains, but it forces the House to say how a petition to change Article 106 would be handled. Third, the executive's distance: Jack Mwiimbu's line that the executive will not involve itself in the legislature's processes is consistent, and its critics' reply is that silence from the President is itself a position. What to watch is whether any of the three produces a document, a police reply, a Speaker's ruling or a State House statement, before Wednesday.
