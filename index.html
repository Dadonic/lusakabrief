Economy · Lead story

K284.8 billion and half a point on VAT: Musokotwane's 2027 budget taxes mining dividends at 15%, adds a 39.5% PAYE band and aims the deficit at 3% of GDP

The Minister of Finance and National Planning, Situmbeko Musokotwane, presented a K284.8 billion budget for 2027 to the National Assembly on Friday 6 November, the largest in Zambia's history and 28.4% of GDP. Domestic revenue is to cover K233.7 billion of it, borrowing K41.9 billion and grants K9.2 billion. VAT rises from 16% to 16.5%, a 15% withholding tax lands on mining company dividends, a new 39.5% PAYE band applies to monthly pay above K37,000, and debt service alone takes K67.6 billion.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

The budget's bet: a half-point of VAT now, a 3% deficit later

The 2027 budget raises the tax everyone pays and taxes the dividends the mines have not paid, and it does both to narrow a deficit that has drifted to an expected 5.9% of GDP against a 2.1% target. The government's case is that stabilisation is done and growth comes next: K284.8 billion, 82% of it from domestic revenue, with the borrowing share held to 14.7% and a path to 3% next year and 1.2% by 2029. The critics' case, put first by Fred M'membe, is that the burden lands on consumers through VAT, mobile money levies and a sugar tax while K67.6 billion goes to creditors, more than double what health receives. Three things decide which reading holds. First, whether the K10.8 billion the tax measures are meant to raise actually arrives, since a VAT rise can push trade into the informal sector. Second, copper: the deficit path assumes output rising past a million tonnes in 2027 after a 930,000-tonne year, and prices that have already touched US$14,000. Third, the rains, because the budget pre-funds a drought the government says it expects. The IMF's final approval, which Briefs, citing Bloomberg, reports hangs on the deficit goal, is the external judge.

Analysis

What an observer report can and cannot do, and why Kalaba's question matters

Harry Kalaba's response to the EU mission, accept every finding and then ask what the observers intend to do about them, puts a question that both sides of Zambia's election argument have reason to answer. For the opposition and the church monitors, the report's 13 recommendations, on ZNBC airtime, the campaign timetable, police conduct and the suspension of counting, are a record they can hold up; but observers have no enforcement power, and Mr Kalaba's point that reports since 1996 have repeated themselves is hard to dispute. For the government, the answer it has given, that observers cannot see what the police and intelligence services can, is a reason to discount the findings on security, but it does not address the airtime or timetable findings, which rest on public data. What to watch is whether any recommendation becomes a bill, a broadcasting policy or an ECZ rule change before the next electoral cycle, and whether the parties that have now received the report, Citizens First first among them, put the recommendations to Parliament and the Commission in writing. The report's value will be measured in those documents, not in the week's statements.

Analysis

A retirement without a reason, and the question it leaves the ministry

Dr Paul Chibwe's early retirement in the national interest is, on what has been published, an administrative act without a stated cause. Retirement in the national interest is a lawful instrument, and the ministry may have grounds it has not yet given; Mr M'membe's own statement lists other restructuring moves at the ministry and its agencies this year, and the budget has just promised 3,125 new health workers, so a reorganisation is one possible explanation. But the timing and the target invite the other reading, which Fred M'membe and Brian Mushimba have already given: that the president of the doctors' association, who spent the year pressing for the recruitment of volunteers already in the hospitals, has been removed for pressing. Two things would settle it. A written reason from the ministry or the Health Services Commission, which neither has given on any page read for this edition, and a clear statement of what happens to the association's recruitment demand now that its leader is outside the service. Until then the ministry carries the burden of the silence, and the doctors who were watching Dr Chibwe's case will draw their own conclusion about what speaking up costs.
