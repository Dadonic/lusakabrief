Courts · Lead story Developing

A former ambassador to Switzerland is charged with treason: police say Martha Mwitumwa, 59, plotted with persons unknown between November 2025 and 23 October 2026

The Zambia Police Service said on Monday 9 November that it had arrested and charged Martha Lungu Mwitumwa, who represented Zambia in Geneva from 2019, with treason under Section 43(1) of the Penal Code. She is in custody awaiting a court appearance. The charge is an allegation; she has not entered a plea and no response from her or a lawyer has been reported. She becomes the latest person charged with treason since the August election, after the 18 accused in the Mundubile case.

'There are no perfect democracies': the EU and Zambia hold the dialogue Lusaka asked for in September, and Haimbe asks to comment on the observers' report before it is published

At the second EU–Zambia Extraordinary Partnership Dialogue in Lusaka on Monday 9 November, the EU's Africa director, Francesca Di Mauro, said electoral grievances should be settled through fair and impartial courts and offered support for a national process of dialogue and reconciliation. Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

A treason charge is the start of a process, not the end of one

The police statement on Martha Mwitumwa is short and its gaps are the point. It names an offence, a period of almost a year and a place; it does not name the plan, the alleged accomplices or the evidence. That is normal at the charging stage, but it means the public has, so far, only the State's label. The 18 people charged in August are still waiting for a High Court ruling on bail after a hearing that was adjourned repeatedly, so the next stage for her may be slow. Two readings will compete. The government's, restated by Dr Andeleki on Friday, is that the law is being enforced against people who planned to take power unlawfully, and that opposition membership is not immunity. Critics', set out by Human Rights Watch this month, is that treason is being used broadly against people connected to the losing side. Monday's charge does not settle that argument; the particulars, when they are read in court, will tell more. Watch for the date of her first appearance, whether co-accused are named, and whether the August detention at a campaign media centre turns out to be part of the same file.

Analysis

Both sides left the EU dialogue with what they came for

Zambia asked for Monday's meeting in September, when the European Parliament's resolution landed before the observers' final report. The request was a way of moving the argument from a chamber in Strasbourg, where Lusaka has no vote, to a table in Lusaka, where it is a party. On that measure it worked. Mr Haimbe secured a commitment to work through the mission's recommendations before 2031, a framing that treats the findings as technical improvements, and he asked for the government's comments to be heard before the final report is published. The EU, for its part, said in public and in Lusaka that grievances must go through fair and impartial courts, that it remains concerned about civic space, and that it will support a process of dialogue and reconciliation. Neither statement, as reported, mentioned the Mundubile trial, which the mission said last week it would follow and which the government spokesman says is none of its business. That silence is the thing to watch: whether the final report, and the EU's response to the treason cases, stay inside the frame agreed on Monday or step outside it.

Analysis

Ngonye Falls has arrangers; the number to watch is the one the reports disagree on

A mandate letter is not money. What Western Power Company signed in Livingstone is an agreement that Stanbic and the DBSA will go and find the debt, and the developer's own description, the last step before financial close, is also an admission that close has not happened. The project has been in development long enough to have earned a sustainability certification and lost none of its logic: a 180MW plant in a province fed by a single 66kV line from 1972 would change what is possible there, from mining to household supply. Two things are unresolved. The first is the price: one report says US$600 million, another US$800 million, and the gap matters to how much debt must be raised and at what tariff ZESCO or private offtakers will pay. The second is timing: construction from 2027 and just under four years to build puts first power around 2031, after the next election and well after the current deficit. Watch for the financial close announcement the developer says is months away, the offtake arrangements, and whether the transmission line is financed with the plant or separately.
