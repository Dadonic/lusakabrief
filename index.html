Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

Where a chant ends and a rally begins

The ministry's worry is not invented: a month after a disputed election, with two opposition figures in detention, a stadium chant can be both symptom and spark, and CAF's neutrality rules — written for exactly this — exist to be cited. FAZ is right that the right to expression travels with the rulebook. The critics' case is also real: 'Salt Sana' rang through these same stadiums before the election with no ministry statement in reply, and M'membe's thermometer line lands because selective alarm is its own confession. What would settle the argument is not louder statements but a published, content-neutral stadium rule — what conduct is banned, what sanction follows, who decides — applied to 'Pe Lampi' and 'Bally' alike. Until then, every floodlight flicker will be read as politics, and every silence as favour. The clubs, told to control their supporters, will want the rule in writing before the next home game.

Analysis

What Tuesday can and cannot settle

Two arguments converge tomorrow. In Solwezi, Magistrate Mukonde decides geography: whether a sedition case built on Facebook livestreams, which the State accepts were made in Lusaka, can be heard hundreds of kilometres to the north-west. A ruling for the defence moves the file to the capital; a ruling for the State could finally produce a plea, several court appearances after the first. What it cannot settle is the substance — the charges stand or fall at trial, not at the venue fight. Nor does it touch the heavier treason matter, which awaits the High Court on its own clock. The DPP's Shamwana precedent — eleven months to commence, in 1985 — is an answer about pace, not place. The critics' counter is custody: every month of process is a month of detention for the accused. Both things are true, which is why Tuesday's ruling, whatever it says, will be read as a verdict on more than jurisdiction.

Analysis

The price of predictability

Monthly pricing was meant to keep the pump honest: track the oil price and the kwacha, pass both through, no hidden subsidy. Six months of tax relief bent that logic — the ERB's October board showed the market moving one way and policy another, and a 24-per-cent single-step rise is what 'smooth' looks like when the cushion expires. The case for a longer interval is planning: a bus operator or a farmer can price a quarter, not a midnight. The case against is exposure: if the kwacha slides for two months, a slower board either swallows the gap or springs a bigger jump. The Government's answer, so far, is a review without terms of reference, an excise duty restored and a VAT holiday with a December date — plus an electric-fleet promise that will not move this year's import bill. What the IMF's staff-level arithmetic assumes about the fuel line is the page everyone is waiting to read.
