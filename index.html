Analysis

Budget day: four things to read for in Musokotwane's speech

The 2027 budget reaches the National Assembly on Friday 6 November, a week later than first planned after the Vice-President moved the date because, as News Diggers reported, the government was still working on it. Four things will show what kind of budget it is. First, the Food Reserve Agency: the minister has said the agency should fund next year's purchases from maize sales, so a small Treasury line for the FRA would confirm that the K14.7 billion in farmer arrears was a one-off. Second, the borrowing mix: the second supplementary budget leaned on K18 billion of domestic borrowing, and Moody's upgrade to Caa1 this week will be tested by whether the 2027 financing plan keeps to the concessional-first line the minister set out. Third, the cost of five new ministries, which the minister says can be absorbed without new recruitment. Fourth, fuel: the US$200 million relief has ended and pump prices have risen, so any new levy or subsidy will be read closely. What the speech cannot settle is the drought the government says it is already paying to pre-empt; the numbers on Friday are a bet on next year's rains.

Analysis

Imaginary or not, the term-limits argument is now about who moves first

The government's position on term limits is that there is nothing to discuss: a petition is an opinion, not a policy, and no bill exists. That is accurate as far as the public record goes, and it is why Elvis Nkandu can call the 25 November procession a protest against an imaginary issue. The organisers' position is also coherent: in 2001 the Oasis Declaration opposed a third term before any bill was tabled, and the two-term limit was examined by last year's review committees and retained, so waiting for a draft amendment would mean arguing after the decision is made. The two sides are not disputing facts so much as timing. For the government the question is whether a firm statement that it has no plan to touch Article 106 would end the matter, or would be read as a concession that a plan was considered. For the civil society groups the question is what a march achieves against a proposal nobody in authority has owned. What to watch: whether the police respond to the notification, whether any minister or ruling party official endorses or disowns the 27 October petition by name, and whether the Law Association answers the letter it was sent this week.

Analysis

What moves with the sedition case, and what Friday's bail argument turns on

Judge Musona's order changes the geography of the Mundubile and Zulu cases but not their substance. The sedition charges, two counts under Section 57 of the Penal Code over Facebook posts of 14 August, now go to a subordinate court in Lusaka, where the accused live and were arrested; the defence has said the move suits the State as much as the accused, since witnesses and lawyers no longer travel to Solwezi. Leave to appeal was granted, so the State could still contest the venue. The bail petition is a different matter and the harder one. Thursday's hearing turned not on whether the eighteen should be released but on whether the High Court, sitting on a constitutional petition, can grant bail at all; the State says that belongs in the criminal courts. If the panel agrees, the application fails without the merits being heard and the accused, in custody since 14 August, would have to start again before the trial court. If it disagrees, the arguments about flight risk, witness interference and the length of detention without trial are finally reached. The panel said on 29 October that there would be no more adjournments. Friday at 09:00 is the test of that.
