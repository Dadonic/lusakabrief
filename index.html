'Properly before the court': the High Court rejects the State's objection and clears the way for the 18 treason accused to argue for bail, as the sedition bail plea slips to Monday

A three-judge panel of the Lusaka High Court ruled on Tuesday 10 November that the constitutional bail application by Brian Mundubile, Makebi Zulu and 16 co-accused was properly before it, rejecting the State's argument that it had been started in the wrong registry. Nobody has been released: the ruling only means the application will now be heard. A day earlier, the pair's other bail plea, in the sedition case, was adjourned again after the State said it had a preliminary issue to raise.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

A door opened is not a door walked through

Tuesday's ruling is narrower than the relief around it suggests. The High Court decided only that the 18 treason accused filed their bail application in a form the court can hear; it said nothing about whether they should be freed. That question, resting on Articles 13 and 28 and on how long someone may be held before trial, is still to be argued, and no date has been published. Two things make the next stage worth watching. First, the State has now lost a procedural objection in the treason case while raising a new, undisclosed one in the sedition case, so the pattern the defence complains of, delay by preliminary issue, will be tested again on Monday. Second, the court's reasoning on jurisdiction may settle how future constitutional bail petitions must be brought. The State's case, as argued so far, has been procedural: that the application was brought in the wrong form. The defence's is substantive: that 74 days without a bail hearing is itself the breach. The substantive hearing is where those two readings finally meet.

Analysis

Counting teachers before hiring them is sound; moving 1,558 in a week is the hard part

Two ministers said the same thing from different ends of the system this week: the government does not know where its teachers are. Dr Musokotwane's examples, schools with an establishment of six carrying 25, others with five and only two present, describe a payroll that has drifted away from the classroom. Freezing recruitment until the count is done follows from that, and whatever the education budget is, it will stretch further if it pays people where they are needed. The Copperbelt order is where the policy meets its limits. Transfers are easy to announce and hard to execute: housing in rural districts, spouses' jobs, children's schooling and the Cabinet's own four-year rule all become live questions once 1,558 letters are issued, and the unions have not yet been heard. Watch whether the one-week assessment produces a list, whether the threatened dismissals are carried out, and whether other provinces follow. If the count is honest, it may also show that the shortage is smaller, or larger, than either minister expects.

Analysis

Zambia's power problem has moved from the drawing board to the bank

The number that matters from Mr Kapala's visit to the regulator is not 10,000 but 16,000: there is more signed capacity in power purchase agreements than the 2031 target requires, and most of it is stuck at financial close. That changes what the government has to do. Signing more agreements will not help; persuading lenders that ZESCO and other buyers will pay, over the life of a plant, in a currency whose swings the IEA has flagged, will. The tools exist on paper, a procurement framework, published guidelines and a UK-backed liquidity mechanism, and the ERB chairman's complaint that financiers demand things the framework already covers suggests the gap is partly one of trust and paperwork rather than policy. The meeting with developers should show which projects are close, which are paper, and what guarantee the Treasury can afford, given the fiscal limits the World Bank described last year. The 945 MW added since 2024 shows it can be done; the next 5,000 MW depends on money that has not yet arrived.
