Courts · Lead story Developing

Friday for jurisdiction, Tuesday for liberty: the High Court rules on 20 November whether it can hear Mundubile and Zulu's sedition bail plea, and on 24 November on bail for the 18

The Lusaka High Court heard the State's objection to the sedition bail application on Monday 16 November and reserved its ruling to Friday 20 November at 09:30. The Director of Public Prosecutions argued that filing in Lusaka while the case sat in Solwezi was an abuse of process; the defence relied on Section 123 of the Criminal Procedure Code. News Diggers reports that the separate constitutional bail application by all 18 treason accused will be decided on Tuesday 24 November, the day Mundubile and Zulu are also due to take plea before a magistrate.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

Friday's ruling is about doors, not liberty

It is worth being precise about what the High Court decides on 20 November. The question is not whether Brian Mundubile and Makebi Zulu should be released on the sedition charges; it is whether the Lusaka High Court is the right door to knock on at all. The State's case has a tidy logic: the file now sits with a Lusaka magistrate, the normal route is to ask the trial court first, and a High Court application lodged while the case was still in Solwezi looks like two bites at one cherry. The defence has an equally tidy reply: Section 123 of the Criminal Procedure Code gives the High Court power to grant bail, the Solwezi courts never heard a bail application, so there is nothing to duplicate. If the objection succeeds, the application goes to Magistrate Milumbe and the clock starts again. If it fails, the court moves to the merits of release pending trial. The 24 November constitutional bail ruling for all 18 is the larger event, because it goes to the length of detention itself. Watch, too, whether plea is finally taken that day, or whether a further adjournment follows.

Analysis

What LAZ can and cannot do about Article 106

The Law Association's promise of litigation changes the register of the term-limit argument from rallies to pleadings, but its reach should not be overstated. Parliament may amend Article 106 by the two-thirds route in Article 79; no court can stop MPs voting. What LAZ can do is test the process in court: above all whether the public consultation the Constitutional Court stressed in its June 2025 judgment on Bill 7 has taken place, and whether a change could lawfully benefit a sitting president. Supporters of removal have a case to make too. Boas Kanyama's consortium says the ballot, not a clause, should decide who governs, and that a petition to Parliament is the constitutional way to ask. The government says it is standing aside, and the Justice Minister's assurance of non-interference is now being quoted approvingly by LAZ itself. The critics' case rests on 2001, on four constitutional review bodies that kept two terms, and on the question Fred M'membe keeps asking: who is paying for 63,151 marchers and 500 police officers. What to watch is procedural: whether the Assembly tables the petitions, whether any MP sponsors a bill, and whether LAZ holds the extraordinary meeting its members have requisitioned.

Analysis

The UNDP's test for the 2027 budget

James Wakiaga's intervention is polite and pointed. The UNDP is not disputing that the Treasury needs revenue; it is asking that each measure be weighed by who pays. On the government's side the case is real: the budget's stated aim is to broaden the tax base and raise domestic revenue, an El Niño season has to be financed, and the stability the UNDP itself credits was bought with discipline. Broad, low-rate levies on mobile money and VAT are efficient precisely because almost everyone pays them. On the other side, the Mobile Money Association, the farmers' union and now the UNDP make the same point from different angles: a levy that doubles on a K150 transfer falls hardest on the people drawn into formal finance by its cheapness, and a passport at K1,000 is a 50 per cent increase on a document many need for work. The UNDP's suggested compromise, keep basic foods zero-rated and raise social cash transfers to match, can still be made before the measures take effect in 2027. The kwacha's drift towards K20.50 adds imported-price pressure on the same households. Watch whether the Treasury answers the UNDP, and what the farmers and the operators do next.
