Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

Botswana has answered for its government. It has not answered Khama

Dr Butale's statement does one thing cleanly: it tells Lusaka that a former president's words are not Gaborone's. That matters, because the remark was made in a formal chamber, by a man who once held the office, about a neighbour whose inauguration Botswana's sitting president attended. Left unanswered, it could have been read as a second channel of Botswana's foreign policy. The statement does not do the other thing critics of Mr Khama wanted, which is to rebut him. The minister defended his right to speak and said nothing, in the remarks reported, about whether the allegations are true; non-interference cuts both ways. So each side can claim something. State House can say Botswana's government has disowned the remark. Mr Khama can say nobody in authority has told him he is wrong, and that he has been neither silenced nor contradicted. What remains open is whether Zambia treats the matter as closed. Watch for any formal exchange between the two foreign ministries, for whether the Ntlo ya Dikgosi returns to the subject, and for whether the next meeting of the two countries' Bi-National Commission is used to say in public what has so far been said to newspapers.

Analysis

13.4% is a share, not a measure of learning, and both sides know it

The teachers' union and the Treasury are arguing about a ratio, and the ratio hides as much as it shows. The union's case is that a fifth of the budget is the recognised benchmark and that 13.4% falls well short. The government's case, implicit in the numbers, is that the allocation rose by more than K5 billion in a year, that school feeding is nearly doubling, and that a budget carrying K67.6 billion of debt service cannot give any sector the share it would in easier times. Both are fair. A share shrinks when the total is swollen by debt payments even if the money for classrooms grows; equally, a rising kwacha figure says nothing about what it buys once enrolment has grown under free education. The more useful question is the one News Diggers put on Monday: whether children are learning. That turns on teacher numbers, class sizes and materials, none of which the reports on the budget itemise. What to watch: the ministry's detailed estimates when they reach the parliamentary committee, how much of the K38.24 billion is salaries, and whether any published measure of learning outcomes is attached to the money.

Analysis

Before the lunch with the mining houses, FAZ's debt needs a single number

A presidential promise to gather the mining companies is the most concrete offer of help the Football Association has had on its old debt, and it came with a fair condition: stop borrowing once it is cleared. But the account of the reception gives the debt as not less than K40 million in the FAZ president's quoted words and as more than K140 million a few paragraphs later. A difference of K100 million is not a rounding matter, and any company asked to contribute will want to know which it is, who is owed, and how the debt arose. There are two reasonable views of the offer itself. One is that a national association with junior teams winning abroad is a good cause for companies that earn their living in Zambia. The other is that an association's solvency should rest on its own accounts, not on appeals brokered at State House, and that corporate money raised this way can come with expectations. What to watch: FAZ's formal letter to the President, whether audited figures for the debt are published, and whether the meeting with the mining houses is given a date.
