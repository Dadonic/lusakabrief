Analysis

The maize bill was a choice as much as a surprise; the borrowing is the part to watch

The second supplementary budget is easy to read as a bumper-harvest accident: farmers grew far more than the Food Reserve Agency planned to buy, and the Treasury must now find the money. But both Thursday's reports make clear that the overshoot — 2.7 million tonnes against a 500,000-tonne target — followed a deliberate decision to stock reserves ahead of a lean 2027 season. The case for that decision is straightforward: a full silo is cheaper than emergency imports in a drought year, and paying farmers keeps rural cash flowing. The case against is in the financing table. K18 billion of the K19.9 billion is to come from extra domestic borrowing, which competes with private borrowers for the same kwacha and arrives while the Government is also asking lenders to back a 'growth, growth, growth' agenda. What is still unknown is when farmers will actually be paid, what the revised Annual Borrowing Plan will cost, and what the roughly K1.5 billion not itemised in either report is for. Watch the Expanded Planning and Budgeting Committee's report, and whether any MP presses on the first two questions.

Analysis

Two readings of the same country: the Rule of Law Index and the view from Monze East

Thursday offered Zambians two sharply different accounts of their democracy within 24 hours. The Chapter One Foundation read the World Justice Project's 2026 index as evidence that authoritarian practices are 'becoming entrenched', citing rankings in the low 100s on fundamental rights, open government and corruption. The day before, Jack Mwiimbu, the immediate past Home Affairs minister, told Parliament that the real failure lay with churches, civil society and the opposition for questioning lawful arrests and staying silent when the President was abused. Neither side was answering the other directly, and that is the gap to notice. The index measures perceptions and expert assessments of institutions over time; Mr Mwiimbu's speech addressed the conduct of particular critics. Both can be true at once: a government can act lawfully in individual cases while the institutions around it are judged to be weakening. What is not yet known is Zambia's overall score and how far it moved, which neither report gave, and whether the Government will respond to the index on its merits. Watch for a formal reaction from the Ministry of Justice and for how Parliament's new opposition leadership handles the question.

Analysis

The gold gap is real in the data; whether Kyalo can close it depends on the price it pays

ZCCM-IH's London pitch rests on a striking contrast: foreign customs records showing well over a billion dollars of Zambian gold arriving in Dubai in a single year, against declared national output of around 127,000 ounces. The mirror-trade data that Africa Briefing traced in September support the scale of the discrepancy, but the same report's caution deserves equal weight — valuation, timing and transit routes can inflate such gaps, and not every undeclared ounce is a smuggled one. The formalisation model is borrowed from Ghana's Gold Board, whose artisanal exports have risen sharply, and the logic is sound: give licensed small-scale miners a legal buyer who pays promptly in kwacha, and route the metal to the central bank. The unanswered question is price. Africa Briefing's own test is the right one: competitive prices, reliable payments, accessible buying centres and licensing rules that do not push small operators back into informal networks. Still unknown are Kyalo's buying prices, its volumes and start date, how many licensed miners it can reach, and the Bank of Zambia's own view on taking the metal into reserves. Those are the details to watch for before judging whether the pit-to-vault chain is a policy or a slogan.
