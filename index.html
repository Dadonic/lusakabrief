Opinion

One sentence could end it

The strangest feature of Zambia's third-term debate is that it has a sponsor but no owner. A backbencher says two terms are not enough; a party lawyer says the clips were clipped; the old guard warns against tailoring the Constitution around one man — and the one man has said nothing. Today he has the podium that settles these things. Article 106 is blunt: twice elected, never again. If the President believes that, he can kill the speculation before lunch with a single declarative sentence, and earn the double benefit of looking like a constitutionalist while spending nothing. If he does not say it, the debate will continue — and every week it continues, the suspicion grows that someone at State House finds it useful. The First Session of the 14th Assembly deserves to open on policy: energy, the budget, the cost of living. Give the country the sentence, and then the speech.

Opinion

The number on the bureau board

Copper is above US$14,600 a tonne, the World Bank has upgraded us, and the kwacha has spent a month sliding back toward twenty to the dollar. None of these facts cancels the others — but they do describe the two economies Zambians inhabit. In the ledger economy, exports boom and forecasters applaud. In the kitchen economy, the fuel pump and the mealie-meal shelf read the exchange rate, not the LME ticker. The analyst who says the kwacha 'should be K10' is making a polemic, not a forecast, but his polemic names a real puzzle: why record export receipts do not reach the currency. The honest answer — import hunger, debt service, a firm dollar — is structural, which is another way of saying it will not be fixed by speeches. The Bank of Zambia's settlement directive and yuan tax payments were the slow medicine. Patience is a policy too, but it is the one households are asked to supply.

Opinion

Two price tags, one signature

Yesterday Zambia signed a health pact that Zambian officials had spent the week pricing at US$3.6 billion — and Washington priced, on signing, at US$2.49 billion. A billion-dollar gap between the podium figure and the paper figure is not a rounding error; it is a demonstration of why the sceptics' single demand matters. Publish the text. Professor Chilengi's account of it is reassuring — no specimens, no minerals, data confined to monitoring — but reassurance is not verification, and the minister himself said the contentious matters were the ones the public raised. The Government won a genuine negotiation this week; it should not squander the victory on a arithmetic it has not explained. M'membe calls the deal padded chains; the DPP wants disclosure; the clinics just want the drugs that the ZAMMSA scandal put in doubt. All three are answered by the same act: the document, in full, online, this month.
