Analysis

What Nakacinda’s release changes, and what the 2 November ruling could still change

Raphael Nakacinda went into Chimbokaila a year ago as the secretary general of a party in opposition; he comes out into a landscape in which, as this week’s reports describe it, PF supporters march alongside the Tonse-Pamodzi Alliance and the question of who leads the opposition in Parliament has only just been settled by the Speaker. His supporters’ reading, voiced at the gate, is that the sentence was political and that his return reinvigorates the opposition. The ruling party’s reading, as the Zambian Observer summarises it, is that an independent court applied a law that was still in force when the offence was committed. Both can point to the record: the conviction survived appeal, and the law it rested on was repealed in December 2022. What remains live is the seditious-practices count on which the Lusaka Magistrates’ Court rules on 2 November. A finding that there is a case to answer puts him back in the dock within weeks of his release; a finding that there is not would leave him without a pending case for the first time in years. Watch, too, for whether the PF formally restores him to his full duties, and for any statement from the party or the Government, since neither had published one by Thursday morning.

Analysis

The fertiliser plant is a bet on import substitution. Three things will tell if it pays

A US$1.6 billion expansion that takes a single company from 7,400 to 10,000 direct jobs fits the Government’s stated logic: grow maize to ten million tonnes, and do it with fertiliser made in Chilanga rather than imported. Whether the bet pays will show up in three places. First, the price farmers actually pay: import substitution lowers input costs only if domestic urea is sold below the landed import price, and none of this week’s reports says what UCF will charge, or on what terms it supplies the Farmer Input Support Programme. Second, the feedstock: the President’s remark, reported by Lusaka Times, that coal can produce the ‘white stuff’ points to a coal-based process, and the reports do not address what that means for emissions or for coal supply. Third, the timetable: the first phase has only begun trial production, according to the Chinese ambassador, and the company’s US$5 billion-by-2030 commitment is a pledge rather than a contract. The President’s warning that he will be ‘ruthless’ with officials who delay approvals is aimed at the third of these. The first two will decide whether the plant changes what a bag of fertiliser costs a smallholder.

Analysis

Why a drill in Milenge matters more than a case in Abuja

Nigeria’s first Ebola case is alarming because of distance: the virus travelled from eastern DRC to a private clinic in Abuja before anyone tested for it. Zambia’s risk runs the other way — not distance but proximity. Luapula shares border communities with the DRC, as its permanent secretary noted this week, and the outbreak declared on 15 May has already produced cases in Kenya and now Nigeria. That is why this week’s simulation in Milenge matters more to Zambians than the headline from Abuja. The question the exercise was built to answer is simple: how many hours between a suspected case in a border village and a laboratory result? Mobile laboratories, if they work as intended, shorten that gap, and the US$1.7 million of US-funded units are the tool. What the reports do not yet tell us is the result of the drill — where the gaps were, and what will be fixed. Nor do they say whether monitoring at Zambia’s border posts has been stepped up since the Nigerian confirmation, which WHO’s guidance on points of entry would support even as it advises against trade or travel restrictions. Those are the follow-ups to watch for from ZNPHI and the Ministry of Health.
