Politics · Lead story

'Overwhelmingly biased': the EU observers' final report hands Zambia 13 recommendations, and the government replies that observers cannot see what the police can

The EU Election Observation Mission published its final report on the 13 August general elections on Tuesday 3 November: 13 recommendations, eight of them priority. It found a competitive choice and efficient polling staff, but late legal changes, police bias, cadreism and incumbency advantage that distorted the playing field, a state broadcaster that gave the ruling party over 90 per cent of political airtime, and a six-hour halt in counting and court closures that curtailed transparency. Information Minister Elvis Nkandu replied the same day.

Opinion & Analysis

Analysis

Declared, not elected: what Thursday settles in Mandevu and what it leaves open

By Thursday evening Mandevu should have a Member of Parliament who received no votes, and the arguments about how that happened will not be over. The Electoral Commission's position is narrow and procedural: three candidates withdrew, one remains, and the Constitution provides for the one to be declared. The objections run on two tracks that should be kept apart. The first, from the Tonse Alliance, is political: that the withdrawals were not free, and that a declaration built on them rewards whoever applied the pressure. The Commission has not said whether it received complaints. That dispute is about facts nobody has yet established. The second track, Kasonde Mwenda's, is legal and goes back further: whether the Commission had any power on 28 August to abandon the original Mandevu process and order a fresh one, or whether that was a question only the High Court could decide. If a judge grants leave, the declaration could be made and later unmade; if not, the case ends quietly. What to watch is the order of events on Thursday: a stay granted before the declaration changes everything, one considered afterwards changes very little in practice.

Analysis

Two benches, one morning: why the bail ruling matters more than the venue ruling

Thursday puts Brian Mundubile and Makebi Zulu before two courts at once, and the two decisions are not of equal weight. The Solwezi question, whether a sedition case over two Facebook posts is tried in North-Western Province or in Lusaka, is about convenience, witnesses and, the defence would say, the optics of trying a Lusaka matter in another province. Whatever the answer, a trial follows somewhere. The Lusaka question is about liberty. Eighteen people have been held at Mukobeko on treason and terrorism-related allegations while the State has not yet set a date for the substantive case, and the three-judge panel has already said it will not adjourn again. Constitutional bail is not a verdict on guilt; it is a finding about whether continued detention before trial is justified. The State's case is that the allegations are grave and its preparation thorough; the applicants' is that delay itself is the injustice and that the prosecution's own manual says so. What to watch is less the headline outcome than the reasoning, and whether the panel rules on the papers or hears argument first. Either way, a date for the petition itself comes next.

Analysis

Reading Friday's budget: the maize bill, the borrowing plan and the rating

The 2027 budget arrives with its framing already written. Cabinet has used the words stability and prudence; Moody's has moved the rating from Caa2 to Caa1; and the Finance Minister has told MPs there will be no third supplementary budget this year. The harder reading is in what the supplementary estimates reveal. Of K19.9 billion in extra 2026 spending, K14.7 billion is owed to farmers who sold 2.7 million tonnes of maize to a Food Reserve Agency that planned to buy 500,000, and K18 billion of the financing is domestic borrowing. The minister calls this spending ahead of time against a forecast drought, and says the agency must now fund its purchases from maize sales rather than the Treasury. The strongest case for that approach is that a full granary in a dry year is worth more than the interest; the strongest case against is that domestic borrowing on that scale crowds out credit and that an agency funded by its own sales has every incentive to sell dear in a lean season. Friday's numbers to watch are the FRA line, the domestic financing figure and whether the five new ministries really cost nothing extra.
